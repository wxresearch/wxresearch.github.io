Should Engineers Copy Nature—or Try to Outperform It?

Evolution has spent millions of years developing wings, fins, adhesives, and other extraordinary structures. But nature and engineers are not necessarily solving the same problems.

Winnie Xiong · 6 min read · 2026

A kingfisher can dive into water with remarkably little splash. A gecko can run across a ceiling. Birds remain airborne using structures made from little more than bone, muscle, skin, and feathers.

Achievements like these make nature seem like an enormous engineering laboratory—one that has been running experiments for millions of years. It is therefore tempting to assume that when engineers face a difficult mechanical problem, evolution has probably already discovered the best solution.

But that conclusion goes too far.

Nature is an extraordinary source of engineering ideas, but it is not a catalogue of perfect designs. Evolution and engineering operate under different constraints, pursue different objectives, and have access to very different materials and methods.

The most productive form of biomimicry is therefore not simply copying biological structures. It is understanding why a biological solution works and then deciding whether its underlying principle can be improved, adapted, or even abandoned.

Nature can reveal solutions engineers might overlook

Biomimicry is especially valuable when a biological system solves a problem in a way that would not be obvious from conventional engineering approaches.

Gecko adhesion is a striking example.

Geckos can climb smooth walls and move across ceilings without glue or suction. Their feet contain millions of microscopic hair-like structures known as setae, which divide into even smaller structures and allow extremely close contact with surfaces.

Research into this mechanism has helped inspire synthetic dry adhesives and other technologies based on similar physical principles.

The importance of the gecko is not that engineers should literally manufacture artificial gecko feet. What matters is the principle revealed by the biological system: a large number of extremely small contact points can collectively produce strong, reversible adhesion.

That principle can then be separated from the animal itself and used in very different contexts, including robotic grippers, climbing robots, and reusable adhesive surfaces.

This illustrates one of biomimicry's greatest strengths. Nature can expose a physical strategy that engineers had not previously considered.

Evolution does not produce “perfect” designs

However, the existence of an effective biological structure does not mean that it represents the best theoretically possible solution.

Natural selection does not design organisms from scratch. Evolution modifies existing organisms through inherited variation, meaning that every new structure must develop from something that already exists.

Biological systems are therefore shaped by historical, genetic, developmental, and environmental constraints. Traits can also involve compromises between competing functions rather than optimization for one specific task.

Consider a bird's wing.

From an aerodynamic perspective, its main purpose might appear to be generating lift and controlling flight. But a biological wing must satisfy many additional requirements. It must grow from living tissue, receive blood, contain nerves, connect to muscles, withstand repeated stresses, repair damage, remain sufficiently light, and ultimately contribute to the animal's survival and reproduction.

Evolution cannot simply replace bone and feathers with carbon-fiber composites because those materials are not available to biological development.

An aircraft designer works under a completely different set of constraints.

Aircraft wings can incorporate aluminum alloys, composite materials, hydraulic systems, electronic sensors, turbines, and computer-controlled surfaces. They do not need to grow, metabolize nutrients, reproduce, or heal in the same way living tissue does.

This difference matters because it means that asking whether an aircraft wing is “better” than a bird's wing is not especially meaningful without first defining what better means.

A bird's wing is extraordinarily effective for a lightweight, self-maintaining living organism. An aircraft wing is extraordinarily effective at moving large numbers of people and enormous quantities of cargo over thousands of kilometres.

Each is adapted to a different problem.

Engineering can sometimes outperform biology

Once engineers are freed from biological constraints, they can create systems that exceed natural organisms in particular measurements.

No bird can carry hundreds of passengers across an ocean at the speed of a commercial jet. No biological muscle can continuously produce the thrust of a modern jet engine. Human-made structures can also operate in environments in which normal organisms could not survive.

This does not mean engineering has “defeated” evolution.

Instead, it shows that optimization depends on the objective.

Evolution tends to favour traits that improve reproductive success under particular environmental conditions. Engineers, by contrast, can deliberately define a narrow target: maximize speed, minimize drag, carry a particular load, withstand a particular temperature, or reduce manufacturing cost.

When the objective changes, the best design may change as well.

This is why copying nature too literally can actually restrict engineering creativity.

The goal of biomimicry is not necessarily imitation

The Biomimicry Institute describes biomimicry as learning from and applying strategies found in nature to human design challenges. The emphasis on learning from nature is important.

Engineers can extract a useful mechanism without reproducing the entire biological system that produced it.

Research on fish-inspired propulsion illustrates this distinction. Fish generate thrust through coordinated movements of their bodies and fins. Researchers have studied these movements and developed flexible artificial fins that reproduce useful aspects of biological swimming.

But an engineered underwater vehicle does not need to become an artificial fish.

An engineer might copy the flexibility of a fin while changing its material. The shape might be modified for a particular speed range. Biological muscle can be replaced with electric motors. Sensors and control algorithms can provide behaviours that do not exist in the original organism.

The biological structure therefore becomes a starting hypothesis:

What physical principle makes this system effective?

Once that question has been answered, engineering can begin.

Nature is a library, not a blueprint

The most useful relationship between biology and engineering may therefore lie somewhere between imitation and independence.

Ignoring nature would be wasteful. Evolution has explored an enormous range of structures, materials, locomotion strategies, sensing mechanisms, and survival strategies. Wings, fins, shells, bones, leaves, spider silk, insect eyes, and gecko feet can all suggest approaches that engineers might never have discovered by starting from conventional designs alone.

But blindly copying nature would be equally limiting.

Evolution does not have access to every possible material or manufacturing process, nor does it optimize organisms for the same purposes engineers care about. Biological systems also carry historical compromises that engineered systems may not need.

Nature should therefore be treated less as a blueprint and more as a library of experiments.

The engineer's task is not simply to ask:

How can I copy what nature has made?

Why does nature's solution work?

What constraints produced that solution?

Which parts of the mechanism are useful for my problem?

And if my constraints are different, can I design something better?

Biomimicry is most powerful when it leads not to imitation, but to understanding. Evolution can provide the inspiration. Engineering still has to decide what to do with it.
